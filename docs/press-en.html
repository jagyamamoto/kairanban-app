Press & Media

kairanban-app is a free, open-source web app (PWA) for Japan's neighborhood associations (chōnaikai), developed and published by Jag Project, LLC as a flagship project to test what "digital self-governance" can look like in the age of generative AI. It digitizes the kairanban — the paper circular board that has been passed from door to door in Japanese neighborhoods for decades. The story in one paragraph

In the country that recently declared victory in its "war on floppy disks," another analog institution survives: the paper kairanban, a clipboard of notices that takes a week or more to circle a neighborhood. An active neighborhood-association officer — who had produced and directed software projects for years but had not written code since BASIC in junior high school — directed a generative AI (Claude Code) to build a replacement, reached a public beta in about two weeks, and released it as free open source. Electronic circular boards are becoming a market in Japan, mostly as monthly subscriptions that drain association fees out of the community. This app is designed the other way around: it runs on free-tier infrastructure, and if anyone is paid to maintain it, it can be the association's own IT-literate members or local businesses — so the money circulates inside the neighborhood. The developer proposed it to his own association in Koto ward, Tokyo, and is now recruiting the first partner association to test it in real use. Every failure along the way — including a 20-out-of-100 score for the first onboarding guide — is published.

FAQ

Q: It is free. How does it sustain itself?

A: The app is free, with no plans to charge. kairanban-app is our verification project: what we gain is first-hand knowledge of AI-era software development and of "digital self-governance" in practice.

Q: What about personal data?

A: Each association hosts its own member data on its own (free-tier) infrastructure; we hold none of it. Members join with the association's approval, and the app deliberately has no feature for members to connect one-to-one. Before publication, we ran optical character recognition over all 33 published images and machine-checked them against the database — zero instances of personal data.

Q: Why give it away?

A: A circular board that keeps association fees inside the community can only exist as free open source. We want associations across Japan — and anyone facing similar problems elsewhere — to use it freely and keep the know-how local.
